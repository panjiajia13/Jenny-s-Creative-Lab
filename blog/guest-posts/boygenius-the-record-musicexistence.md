## Content (paste into the body)

There's a myth the music industry loves: the lone boy genius, the tortured male visionary who descends from the mountain with a masterpiece. boygenius named themselves after that myth so they could take it apart. Julien Baker, Phoebe Bridgers and Lucy Dacus were each already the kind of songwriter other songwriters envy — three separate careers, three separate spotlights — when they realised the more radical move was to stand in one place together.

The band began almost by accident. Booked on a 2018 tour as three solo acts, they started writing together to pass the time and came away with a self-titled EP that felt less like a side project than a secret. Then they went quiet for five years. When they returned in 2023 with *the record*, it arrived like a reunion you didn't know you'd been waiting for — three friends who'd spent half a decade getting even better, pouring all of it into a debut album about the thing that made them a band in the first place: each other.

*the record* opens with a dare. "Without You Without Them" is ninety seconds of nothing but their three voices braided together, no instruments to hide behind — a hymn to lineage and to one another that essentially says, *listen to how well these people already fit*. From there the album refuses to settle into one mode. "$20" is a Julien Baker firework, all coiled tension until Bridgers is screaming the word "money" into the rafters. "Emily I'm Sorry" is Bridgers at her most dream-lit and self-lacerating. And "True Blue," led by Dacus, might be the warmest song any of them has written — a plainspoken love letter to a friendship, built on the almost unbearable comfort of the line, *it feels good to be known so well*.

What lifts the album above the sum of three great writers is how generously they hand the microphone around. On "Cool About It," they take a verse each over Simon & Garfunkel fingerpicking, each confessing to the small humiliations of pretending you're fine in front of someone you still love. "Not Strong Enough" turns self-loathing into something you can dance to, its "always an angel, never a god" refrain looping at the end until the whole band is singing it as a round — private misery made communal, which is more or less the entire point of boygenius. They take turns being the strong one.

The record is unafraid of its own grandeur — the churn of "Satanist," the widescreen ache of "Anti-Curse" — but it saves its quietest gesture for last. "Letter to an Old Poet" reaches back to "Me & My Dog," the standout from that 2018 EP, and rewrites it: where the younger song longed to vanish alongside someone, the older one finally chooses to leave. It's a band closing a loop in real time, five years of growing up compressed into a single melodic callback. You have to have loved the first song to feel the second one land — and that's the kind of trust this album asks of you, then rewards.

There's a reason boygenius became a genuine cultural event: the restaged Nirvana *Rolling Stone* cover, the Kristen Stewart–directed short film, the sweep of the rock and alternative categories at the 2024 Grammys. But strip the mythology away and what's left is stranger and more moving — three people at the top of their field deciding that being *known* beats being worshipped, and making an album that proves it. In an era of alternative indie music in love with the solitary confessional, *the record* argues for the opposite: that the truest version of yourself might be the one your friends can already see.

It's why boygenius sits so near the top of my running list of the [best alternative indie bands worth hearing right now](https://jenn.ghost.io/indie-alternative-music-bands/). Start with "Not Strong Enough," then let the whole thing play in order — it was built to be heard as one long conversation, and it remains one of the quietly radical records of the decade.
